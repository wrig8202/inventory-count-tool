Behind the interface / project write-up

A better count starts
with a better
understanding of the work.

I built this inventory workflow while working as the planner who needed its output. The purpose was practical: establish a trustworthy starting point every morning, without repeating avoidable manual work.

Real manufacturing origin · fictional pizzeria demonstration · AI-assisted development

Situation

The system existed.
The confidence didn’t.

Material movements were not consistently captured in the system. Daily planning still needed a usable inventory position, which meant walking the floor, counting physical stock, and reconciling the result by hand.

The original process took roughly two hours. It combined paper tallies, partial-container estimates, calculator work, and spreadsheet entry. Inventory kept moving during the count, so the output was an observation window—not a perfectly simultaneous snapshot.

The deeper problem was consistency. Two people could use different assumptions and arrive at different totals. A discrepancy also needed to be traced back to a place and material before anyone could correct it.

Task

Make the necessary work
repeatable and actionable.

My goal was to reduce counting and reconciliation effort while producing a dependable planning input. Removing the count altogether would require fixing the upstream transaction process; this tool makes the current reality more manageable and makes its failures easier to see.

  • Guide the physical route: reduce hunting, missed locations, and uncertainty about what has already been checked.
  • Standardize the math: convert container quantities using explicit standards rather than repeated mental arithmetic.
  • Explain differences: retain expected stock, actual stock, location, and discrepancy.
  • Support the next decision: compare counted inventory with daily demand and keep inbound stock separate until receipt.
Action

Design around the count,
then connect it to planning.

I translated the physical workflow into a configurable route and a consistent entry method. Expected quantities become prompts for verification; they are not accepted as physical stock until counted.

Friction observedDesign response
Repeated unit conversionsContainer standards convert quantities automatically, with the arithmetic visible.
Unclear progressA defined route and saved count status show what is complete.

Inbound mistaken for available stockDelivery quantities remain separate until received; each load can be received once.

The app runs locally in the browser. There is no account or server requirement. The existing counting engine supports reconciliation, demand, receipts, exports, and discrepancy history; the guided demo gives a first-time visitor a smaller route through that same engine.

Result

A usable inventory picture,
with its exceptions attached.

The workflow combines physical entry, conversion, and discrepancy logging instead of requiring separate tallying and transcription steps. Counts can be exported for planning, and the mismatches provide a focused correction list.

Time baseline: approximately two hours per daily count, as recorded in the original case study. A measured after-time has not yet been supplied, so this portfolio does not claim a verified reduction percentage. The time-savings calculator makes hypothetical savings explicit and editable.

The result is not a claim that software repaired transaction discipline. The tool supports the daily verification process and reveals where the underlying inventory process needs attention.

Why a pizzeria?

Familiar ingredients.
The same engineering problem.

Last Slice is a fictional business used to explain the work without publishing employer data. Before the first order, a kitchen lead discovers that the system shows more cheese than exists, flour is correct, and sauce is present but unrecorded.

The three-stop route uses synthetic observations: mozzarella at 4 kg versus 12 kg recorded, flour at 100 kg versus 100 kg, and sauce at 9 kg versus zero. A sample delivery is expected the next morning; it is not counted as on-hand today.

All products, locations, demand, and receipt quantities are fictional. The short demo duration is not a measurement of a real full inventory count.

My contribution & reflection

The value is in the
operational judgment.

My work connects process observation, counting standards, route design, exception handling, and planning needs. AI-assisted development helped implement the interface and logic; the operational problem, workflow decisions, and responsibility for validation remain mine.

A useful next measurement is a matched before-and-after time study: the same count scope, comparable conditions, and a split between walking, counting, reconciliation, and transcription. Separately, tracking recurring discrepancies can show where process fixes reduce the need for manual verification.
